---
title: Geological maps of Kazakhstan: choosing a scale and checking the source
description: How a geological map differs from maps of licences and mineral occurrences, what 1:200 000 and 1:50 000 mean, and what to ask a geologist before using a map.
---

A search for a geological map of Kazakhstan returns materials made for different purposes. Some show rocks and faults; others show deposits or current subsoil use rights. Before using a map to assess an area, establish its purpose, original scale, coverage and date. A point on a map coinciding with an area of interest does not, by itself, establish either a mineral deposit or a right to work there.

## Three maps answer different questions

**A geological map** describes the territory's geology: the distribution and age of rocks, contacts and structures. Colours and symbols only make sense with the legend. The map helps place an area in geological context and form an exploration hypothesis; it does not prove that an economic deposit exists at a particular point. This is consistent with [USGS's description of geological mapping](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/earth-mri/geologic-framework-mapping); Kazakhstan's Geology Committee likewise discusses rocks and faults in its account of [1:50 000 mapping](https://www.gov.kz/memleket/entities/geology/press/news/details/1143562?lang=ru).

**A map of deposits and mineral occurrences** plots known features, often over a geological base. Check what each symbol denotes: a deposit, mineral occurrence, prospective target and historical find are different kinds of information. Ask for the source, date and method of verification. One symbol does not establish ore quality or reserve quantity. If a resource or reserve estimate is given, its reporting standard and date must be stated separately; P1–P3 forecast categories must not be presented as reserves. See our [guide to reserve classifications](/en/insights/reserve-classification-gkz-kazrc-jorc-gbt17766).

**A subsoil use map** addresses which licences, contracts and territories are shown in a government service. According to [Kazakhstan's Ministry of Industry and Construction on the Unified Subsoil Use Platform](https://www.gov.kz/memleket/entities/mps/press/news/details/945522?lang=ru), its interactive map and associated information are used to view areas and completed work. Check an area's status against the official source on the date of a decision: a geological map cannot replace that check. This guide does not determine the legal status of any particular area.

## Choosing a scale

At 1:200 000, 1 centimetre on the original map represents 2 kilometres on the ground; at 1:50 000, it represents 500 metres. A 1:50 000 sheet is therefore usually more useful for examining local geological relationships, while 1:200 000 gives an initial overview of a larger area. Enlarging the images may make both sheets appear the same size on a screen, but it cannot create additional field detail. [USGS explains scale](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/usgsmaps/usgsmaps.html) as the ratio of map distance to ground distance, and the Geology Committee describes a move from regional generalisation to more detailed interpretation at [1:50 000](https://www.gov.kz/memleket/entities/geology/press/news/details/1143562?lang=ru).

To select an area, start with an overview sheet and adjacent territory. Then compare the available larger-scale material for the area of interest. If only a regional map is available, do not turn its lines into precise work boundaries by enlarging a PDF. Scale limits readable detail; survey quality, year and the nature of the original observations also affect reliability.

## Five checks before using a map

1. **Year and version.** Check the dates of surveying, publication and later revisions. A recently uploaded file may rely on an old geological base; new presentation does not mean new field data.
2. **Legend and explanation.** Find definitions of colours, letter codes, line types and confidence levels. These are part of the geological material, not an optional expert appendix. [USGS recommends explaining map units and symbols](https://pubs.usgs.gov/unnumbered/7000088/sta22.pdf).
3. **Coverage.** Compare the sheet boundary with the area you need. If a map is named after a region, check which administrative boundaries that name refers to and whether the full area of interest is shown.
4. **Provenance.** Record the author or institution, work title, original scale and compilation sources. For a scan, establish whether it is georeferenced and how that registration was checked. [A paper published by USGS on citing digital geological maps lists the author, date and scale of the source material](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2000/of00-325/richard.html).
5. **Limits of inference.** Separate observations, interpretation and forecast. A fault, anomaly or mineral occurrence calls for further investigation; it is not a ready conclusion about reserves or an area's prospects.

## What to send a geologist

State the area, your task and the material you already have. For example: “We need to compare two areas for preliminary gold exploration. We have a 1:200 000 geological sheet with its year and legend; which materials would help us check the structure and known occurrences?” Include a source link, sheet name, scale, year, bounds of the area of interest and a specific question. Ask what can be concluded from the available data, what gaps remain and what should be checked in more detailed material or in the field. If you also need an opinion on licences, request a separate check dated to an official source.
