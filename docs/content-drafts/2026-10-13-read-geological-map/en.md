# How to read a geological map: colours, faults and cross-sections

A geological map shows how rocks and geological structures are distributed across an area. Reading it takes more than coloured areas: you also need the legend, scale, labels and the author's notes. The same approach applies to maps of Kazakhstan: first identify what a specific sheet shows, then consider what it may tell you about the area.

If you are still choosing a map, start with our [guide to geological maps of Kazakhstan](/en/insights/geological-map-kazakhstan): a bedrock map, a map of known mineral occurrences and a map of subsoil rights answer different questions.

## 1. Start with the map frame and legend

Find the sheet name, scale, year, coordinate grid and symbols. Keep this information with the map itself: a cropped screenshot can lose important context.

The legend pairs each geological unit with a colour or pattern, a letter code and a description. These may indicate age, rock composition and other features. Read them together: a colour without its label is not enough. The [Wisconsin Geological and Natural History Survey (WGNHS)](https://home.wgnhs.wisc.edu/wisconsin-geology/how-to-read-geologic-map/) recommends this kind of map reading.

Check which map layer you selected as well. For example, the [BGS Geology Viewer](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/map-viewers/bgs-geology-viewer/) distinguishes bedrock from superficial deposits, and its legend changes with the map content. This is a UK teaching example, not a source of data about Kazakhstan.

## 2. Convert the scale into a distance you can picture

At a scale of 1:200,000, one centimetre on the original map represents two kilometres on the ground. At 1:50,000, it represents 500 metres. Four kilometres therefore spans two centimetres and eight centimetres, respectively.

On screen, zooming changes the image size. Use the scale bar or the viewer's measuring tool. Enlarging a scan does not add new observations: a small fault or an individual rock exposure may not have been included in the source material.

## 3. Tell a rock boundary from a fault

A contact is a boundary between geological units. A fault is associated with displacement along a fracture. The two lines may look similar, so compare their symbols with the legend.

A solid, dashed or other line may show confidence, a position beneath overlying deposits or another condition. Do not assume every dashed line means the same thing. The [USGS/FGDC standard for geologic map symbols](https://pubs.usgs.gov/tm/2006/11A02/) describes several variants and their uses; it should not automatically be treated as the legend for a Kazakhstan map sheet.

## 4. Read the orientation symbol

Strike shows the direction of a layer; dip shows the direction and angle at which it slopes. A common symbol may have a short line and a number such as 35° beside it. That is the dip angle, not depth, metal content or layer thickness. Check the legend to identify exactly what was measured and how it is shown: different symbols may refer to layers, fractures or other structures.

## 5. Find the cross-section line

A cross-section shows an interpretation of the structure in a vertical plane along a selected line. First find that line on the map, then compare the units it crosses with the section. Check the horizontal and vertical scales: if they differ, slopes appear differently in the drawing.

A layer drawn as continuing underground does not mean its full shape has been established by drilling. Check which observations and materials the author used.

## A first-pass example

Suppose the area you are reviewing crosses two coloured units and a dashed line. A useful sequence is to identify both units in the legend, interpret the line, check the original scale and find the matching cross-section. The result is a clear description of the geological setting and questions for further checking. The combination of a colour and a fault does not by itself prove a gold deposit.

For reviewing collected maps, samples and reports, see the [geological materials checklist](/en/insights/geological-due-diligence-kazakhstan).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you find gold from a map colour?

No. A colour identifies a geological unit according to the legend. Assessing mineralisation requires more information; the map alone does not confirm reserves.

### Can I use a map without its legend?

For a meaningful review, find the complete sheet and its notes. Guessing what codes and lines mean can lead to the wrong conclusion.

### What should I send a geologist?

Send the complete sheet with its legend, title and source, scale and year, the boundary of the area you are interested in, and a short question. For example: “I want to understand which rocks and structures are shown here and what data would be needed for further review.” You can discuss the task with the team on our [geological services page](/en/services/geological).
